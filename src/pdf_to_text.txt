Review Article What can virtual reality offer to stroke patients? A narrative review of the literature Joanna Szczepa ́nska-Gierachaa, Bła ̇zej Cie ́slikb,∗, Sebastian Rutkowskic, Paweł Kiperd and Andrea Turollad aUniversity School of Physical Education in Wroclaw, Wroclaw, Poland bJan Dlugosz University in Czestochowa, Czestochowa, Poland cOpole University of Technology, Opole, Poland dLaboratory of Neurorehabilitation Technologies, San Camillo IRCCS S.r.l., Venice, Italy Abstract. BACKGROUND: Studies demonstrated the efficacy of virtual reality (VR) as a method supporting the post-stroke neurorehabilitation process by activating motor learning processes. Nevertheless, stroke is frequently accompanied by serious psychological problems including depression, which is associated with an increased risk of mortality, lower post-stroke physical activity, and higher disability in stroke patients. OBJECTIVES: To explore the current use of VR as a method supporting the neuro-rehabilitation process, both in physical and psychological dimensions. METHODS: An exploratory review was conducted with a narrative synthesis. PubMed was used for literature search. Search includes the use of VR in physical rehabilitation, and as support therapy in psychiatric disorders. Both primary research and systematic reviews were included. RESULTS: In neurological disorders rehabilitation, out of 22 studies, 16 concerned stroke survivors. In psychiatric disorders, 44 literature reviews were included. CONCLUSION: The studies confirmed the effectiveness of various forms of VR treatment in the alleviation of psychological andbehavioralproblemsandpsychiatricdisorders.ThereisashortageofVR-basedtechnologicalsolutionsthatwould,besides physical rehabilitation, offer stroke patients therapeutic tools to alleviate psychological disturbance and improve the patient’s mood and motivation. Such solutions will most likely become a field of intensive research in the coming years.

2. Rehabilitation process and virtual reality (VR)

Currently, the attention of many scientists and experts in various fields is drawn to the branch of research devoted to the role of modern technologies and VR in the rehabilitation process. As this review was being written, the PubMed database listed 1772 scientific papers containing key words virtual reality and rehabilitation. After adding a clinical trials filter, 328 studies were found; 228 studies had been identified as randomized controlled trials, which indicates a very high research activity in this area. Further filters were used to find full texts of articles written within Fig. 1. Flowchart of the use of VR in the rehabilitation process. the last five years. Thus, 24 papers were subject to a thorough analysis (Fig. 1). From the 24 selected articles, 22 were related to the treatment of neurological disorders (with 1132 participants), one study (with 30 participants) was related to the treatment of orthopedic impairment, and one study (with 70 participants) was related to geriatric patients. From the 22 studies on neurological disorders, 16 concerned stroke survivors and included a total of 859 patients. All the above studies demonstrated the efficacy of virtual reality as a method supporting the neurorehabilitation process, most likely through enhanced stimulation provided by the artificially generated environment which can activate motor learning processes (Pawel Kiper et al., 2016; Turolla et al., 2013; Zondervan et al., 2016). Within the included trials, the biggest one was a study published by Turolla et al. which involved 376 participants. This study showed that the greatest improvement can be achieved when performing virtual reality after 3 months from stroke onset and in patients with moderate motor deficit (Turolla et al., 2013). Virtual reality seems to be beneficial in improving upper limb function and activities of daily living function when used as an adjunct to usual care (Ballester et al., 2016; Cameir ̃ao, Badia, Duarte, Frisoli, & Verschure, 2012; Paweł Kiper, Agostini, Luque-Moreno, Tonin, & Turolla, 2014; Saposnik et al., 2016; Turolla et al., 2013). The total dose of VR therapy varied between studies. Single sessions lasted from 20 minutes to one hour, and a minimum of 10 sessions of training were provided. The VR treatments were administered from three times a week to five times a week. Different J. Fourteen of the studies used the so-called specialized VR systems (i.e. those created for specific motor disorder) (Ballester et al., 2016; Calabr`o et al., 2017; Cameir ̃ao et al., 2012; Cho, Kim, Lee, & Lee, 2015; Fluet et al., 2015; In, Lee, & Song, 2016; Paweł Kiper et al., 2014; McEwen, Taillon-Hobson, Bilodeau, Sveistrup, & Finestone, 2014; Shin, Bog Park, & Ho Jang, 2015; Shin, Ryu, & Jang, 2014; Standen et al., 2017; Thielbar et al., 2014; Turolla et al., 2013; Zondervan et al., 2016) and two of the studies used commercially available gaming consoles (i.e. Nintendo Wii) (Cho, Lee, & Song, 2012; Saposnik et al., 2016). 3. Psychological aspects in assessing the effects of post-stroke rehabilitation There is a key issue worth considering at this point. All the quoted studies confirmed the efficacy of VR treatment as a method supporting the recovery and rehabilitation process after a stroke. Only one study, when evaluating the effects of treatment, took psychological aspects into consideration. Calabr`o et al. as secondary outcome evaluated depression with the use of the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression. The final assessment showed a significant reduction of depressive symptoms (Calabr`o et al., 2017). Likewise, Zondervan et al. in their initial assessment included the severity of depression (they used the Geriatric Depression Scale to assess the level of depression) but did not evaluate it after the intervention (Zondervan et al., 2016). The lack of interest in psychological aspects of VR treatment after stroke is an astonishing fact, considering that as far back as 20 years ago, the World Health Organization emphasized the necessity to combine the medical and social rehabilitation models, and called this idea a “biopsychosocial” therapeutic approach (Geyh et al., 2004; World Health Organization, 2001). Thus, in 2006 the Helsingborg Declaration on European Stroke Strategies modified the rehabilitation goals, emphasizing that independence in the activities of daily life, as well as the quality of the patients’ life should become an important goal of neurorehabilitation (Kjellstr ̈om, Norrving, & Shatchkute, 2007). There are numerous factors that influence the quality of life of a disabled person, among which mood disorders seem to be one of the key factors (Janus-Laszuk, MirowskaGuzel, Sarzynska-Dlugosz, & Czlonkowska, 2017). Approximately one-third of stroke survivors develop post-stroke depression, and the frequency is highest in the first year after a stroke (Towfighi et al., 2017). Available literature reviews confirm a frequent occurrence of mood disorders in post-stroke patients. Hackett et al. analyzed twelve randomized control trials (N = 1121 subjects) and found that antidepressant medications might be effective in treating post-stroke depression, with a beneficial effect of antidepressants on remission and response, measured as a more than 50% reduction in mood scores (Hackett, Anderson, House, & Xia, 2008). Another review, taking into account five randomized control trials (N = 1078 subjects), suggested that psychosocial therapies could prevent the development of post-stroke depression; however, the studies were limited by heterogeneity in design, analysis, inclusion and exclusion criteria, inadequate concealment of randomization, and high numbers of dropouts (Robinson et al., 2008). Therefore, we undertook a more thorough analysis of the available literature. Having entered the key words stroke and depression in the PubMed database, we received 8579 records, which confirms the increased interest of researchers to study this problem. 1370 records were literature reviews, and 260 were systematic reviews of the literature. The most recent studies, offering also meta-analyses of the selected papers, confirm the link between depression and a significantly increased risk of mortality, lower post-stroke physical activity and higher disability in stroke survivors (Bl ̈ochl, Meissner, & Nestler, 2019; Cai, Mueller, Li, Shen, & Stewart, 2019; Thilarajah et al., 2018). Meta-analysis involving 61 studies (N = 25 488) revealed depression in 33% of patients one year after stroke, with a decline to 25% in the period between one and five years after stroke, to 23% five years after stroke (Hackett & Pickles, 2014). Seven trials (N = 775 subjects) suggested that brief psychosocial interventions could be useful J. Szczepa ́nska-Gieracha et al. / Virtual reality in stroke patients and effective in treatment of post-stroke depression (Alexopoulos et al., 2012; Hackett et al., 2008; Kirkness et al., 2017; Mitchell et al., 2009; Thomas, Walker,Macniven,Haworth,&Lincoln,3).Itwas also proved that there is a link between poor efficacy of physiotherapy and a presence of depressive symptoms, a low level of acceptance of illness and a lack of a sense of self-efficacy in the rehabilitation process (Kobyla ́nska et al., 2018).

4. Virtual therapy and post-stroke depression At this stage of the analysis of the literature we attempted to answer the question whether studies that evaluate the efficacy of VR treatment of post-stroke depression exist. The keywords stroke, depression and virtual reality were used to search the PubMed database. No time restrictions were applied. Ten studies were found (Cameir ̃ao, Faria, Paulino, Alves, & Berm ́udez I Badia, 2016; Choi, Ku, Lim, Kim, & Paik, 2016; De Luca et al., 2017; de Rooij, van de Port, Visser-Meily, & Meijer, 2019; Flynn, Palma, & Bender, 2007; G. House et al., 2016; Gregory House etal.,2016;O’Dell,Lin,&Harrison,9;Shinetal., 2015; Song & Park, 2015), of which four were identified as randomized controlled trials (Choi et al., 2016; de Rooij et al., 2019; Shin et al., 2015; Song & Park, 2015). The most recent (2019) paper is a description of a study protocol of Virtual Reality Training After Stroke Randomized Controlled Trial. The results of this project are yet to be published (de Rooij et al., 2019). In 2016 Choi et al. carried out a randomized, double-blind, controlled trial which included patients with ischemic stroke (Choi et al., 2016). The intervention group received 30 minutes of conventional occupational therapy and 30 minutes of the mobile upper extremity rehabilitation program using a smartphone and a tablet PC (MoU-Rehab). The controls received conventional occupational therapy alone for 1 h per day. Rehabilitation consisted of 10 sessions of therapy, five days a week, for two weeks. A greater improvement in Fugl-Meyer Assessment of the upper extremity, Brunnstr ̈om stage for the arm and the hand and manual muscle testing was observed after treatment with the MoU-Rehab than with conventional therapy. The extent of improvements in the modified Barthel Index, EuroQol-5 Dimension, and Beck Depression Inventory was not significantly different between the two groups. Patients in the experimental group completed the 2-weeks treatment without adverse effects, and they were generally satisfied with the MoU-Rehab (Choi et al., 2016). Two other articles were published in 2015. Both were related to supplementing traditional rehabilitation with VR systems, but the first used a specialized VR system (game-based VR rehabilitation with RehabMasterTM system, D-Gate, Seul, Korea), and the second used Xbox Kinect (Shin et al., 2015; Song & Park, 2015). In the study by Shin et al., the authors investigated whether game-based virtual reality rehabilitation, combined with occupational therapy (20 sessions over four weeks), could improve healthrelated quality of life, mood (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale), and upper extremity function. The were no significant differences between experimental and control group in psychological measurement (Shin et al., 2015). On the other hand, Song and Park analyzed the effects of training using virtual reality games on balance and gait ability, as well as the psychological characteristics of stroke patients, such as depression(BeckDepressionInventory)andinterpersonal relationship by comparing them with the effects of ergometer training. Both groups received training 30 minutes per day, five times per week, for eight weeks. Both groups demonstrated an improvement in mood (p < 0.05), with a greater improvement in the VR group (Song & Park, 2015). At this point, it is worth noting that none of the above virtual rehabilitation systems used any therapeutic techniques that would be specifically developed to relieve emotional disturbance. Most likely, while evaluating the results of rehabilitation carried out using either traditional methods or the VR treatment, the authors assumed that mood improvement may be one of the effects of physical rehabilitation, as this is usually the case when a disabled patient’s functional state improves. However, the literature contains many scientific reports confirming the effectiveness of therapies conducted in virtual environment with regard to various types of psychological, behavioral and psychiatric problems.

It looks as if the experts dealing with the application of VR in medicine and therapy voluntarily chose to belong to either one of two groups: those who had focused on the development of devices/software to assist physical rehabilitation of patients with disabilities, and those who had focused in their work on mental problems in physically fit individuals. And, as demonstrated above, in the case of stroke survivors their mental state is of key importance for the success of treatment and rehabilitation. This is not only the question of improving mood and reducing anxiety, but also of increasing the motivation for long-term rehabilitation, increasing the sense of self-efficacy in the process, and ultimately, of the patient taking over the responsibility for the effects of rehabilitation. Only then can we expect satisfactory and long-lasting results. All the aspects listed above relate to well-documented psychological mechanisms that can be strengthened and reinforced in a certain manner, while at the same time eliminating or attenuating the dysfunctional ones. It is a matter of common knowledge to psychologists, therapists, creators of different forms of virtual therapy, and also to the creators of video games which had long ago ceased to be just a primitive entertainment.

Virtual reality and psychology of total immersion Together with the growing access to computers offering enormous computational powers (both for the game developers and users), the quality of video games and their role in mass culture has been increasing year by year. The global gaming market is worth about 135 billion dollars with annual growth of about 10% and is projected to reach 174 billion dollars in 2021 (Newzoo, 2018). According to the 2016 Newzoo report, over two billion people worldwide play the broadly understood computer games (Newzoo, 2016). One of the most popular platforms for distributing games to PC users is the STEAM application, and data revealed at the Melbourne Games Week show that currently STEAM is used by over 47 million players every day. About 100 million players use the platform less frequently, i.e. once a week (Bailey, 2018). Computer games are universally seen as socially harmful, which is due, among other things, to the presented violence, social isolation of the player and a high risk of addiction. However, as a result of increased spending on this sector of entertainment and in response to the more sophisticated needs of the players, the gaming market has produced many titles of highly educational value which aim to develop the curiosity about the world and stimulate cognitive functions. Full immersion in virtual reality, obtained by separating the user from external stimuli, displaying interactive 3D graphics, providing spatial sound and enabling decision-making in the virtual world provides great opportunities not only for entertainment, but also for therapeutic purposes. This is where the notion of immersion appears. The term refers to a total absorption of the senses and consciousness in the virtual reality. There are several kinds of immersion:

• Skillful immersion, which requires the involvement of attention and skill in order to perform an action, a trick or a combination of moves or punches (typical for arcade or action games). • Strategic immersion, which requires the involvement of intellectual resources and prediction of the consequences of the player’s decisions. • Narrative immersion, known from books and films, when we become involved in the plot of the story we are being told. • Spatial immersion, where the player has the sense of total presence in the virtual world and becomes immersed both in terms of body and mind in the virtual reality.

When all the above kinds of immersion are successfully combined with the interactivity of the virtual reality, we may speak of total immersion. The user becomes a part of the virtually created world, is fully absorbed by the virtual space and involved in the J. Szczepa ́nska-Gieracha et al. / Virtual reality in stroke patients story being told, additionally being able to influence the course of events. The idea of total immersion is also related to the notion of “escapism”, meaning an escape from reality into the world of fiction and illusion. For most users, games are just interactive entertainment with the level of engagement bearing the risk of addiction. This aspect of gaming industry may raise legitimate concerns, as it causes real damage to the lives of many people. However, this technology, if developed for the needs of people with disabilities, suffering from various mental problems, can become an effective tool to support treatment, therapy and rehabilitation in many diseases, including stroke. The goal to be achieved would be a total cut-off of the patient for the duration of the therapy from the hospital environment which is quite often a source of anxiety and pain. This would mean taking the patient, on a virtual journey (story), during which they will realize their resources, retrieve the hidden strengths and begin the fight for regaining functional independence and full recovery. Such a game (story) may incorporate the elements of physical exercise and motor activation to perform a specific task; it may also provide cognitive stimulation, encourage brain plasticity processes, as well as have a strong influence on the player’s (patient’s) mood and the sense of wellbeing. A properly developed therapeutic story will mobilize the patient when necessary and increase the motivation for rehabilitation while encouraging and increasing self-confidence. Most importantly, thanks to the phenomenon of total immersion, it will engage the patient so deeply that they will be able to overcome their own mental and emotional barriers to proceed to the next stage of the game reflecting in the real world the process of rehabilitation.

7. Future research directions The authors of this study believe that the forthcoming years will see intensive development of technologies using VR goggles in both the entertainment industry and in other applications, including rehabilitation and therapy in various types of medical conditions. At present, the development of displays has reached its peak and electronic integrated circuits are finally able to instantly generate images right in front of the user’s eyes. Producers of VR goggles offer new models every year. At present, VR goggles enable the display of high-resolution images with great picture quality (with a very fast refresh rate = 90 Hz). The applied organic light-emitting diode display inside the glasses allows for highly accurate color replication. Numerous sensors placed in the VR helmet (such as a gyroscope, accelerometer, magnetometer) instantly transfer movements of the user’s head into virtual reality. Thanks to special controllers (manipulators), the user’s hand movements are precisely transferred into the VR world. The sensors positioned on the surface of manipulators are detected in space, similarly to the goggles. The hand movement mapping is precise enough to enable automatic computer analyses and monitoring. The projection of the image displayed inside the VR helmet is possible thanks to advanced methods of rendering three-dimensional graphics (Mazurek et al., 2019). Initially, VR glasses were offered with very little content to be displayed inside. Currently, teams of programmers throughout the world are racing each other to create even more refined and sophisticated VR content. Visionaries have obtained a new tool - a set for displaying any created scenarios in an unprecedented quality and form. Thanks to the collaboration between experienced programmers and a multidisciplinary team of experts, it has now become possible to design computer applications for therapeutic purposes. The latest-generation video games engines, so far used only in the entertainment industry, make a perfect base for such projects. They may serve as a great foundation to be “overbuilt” with advanced, precise, tailor-made algorithms necessary to conduct virtual therapy for specific diseases and disorders. Until recently, such engines have been a very expensive part of the whole project, with license prices reaching up to $1 million. Currently, producers have changed the licensing system which is now based either on a so-called success fee (a compensation structure paid to an investment bank for successfully closing a transaction), a nominal fee, or even a free license. In consequence, it is now possible to use state-of-the-art VR technologies also in the treatment and rehabilitation sector, which has never disposed of such large financial resources as the video gaming industry where the profits have been enormous for many years. Today, the quality of the computer-generated image, in real time, is very close to photorealistic quality. The entire experience is made whole by the surround sound effects. Such a system enables its user to achieve total immersion in the virtual world. A team of software developers, graphic designers and J. Szczepa ́nska-Gieracha et al. / Virtual reality in stroke patients musicians is able to create practically any virtual environment so that it is interactive, operated using the above-mentioned controllers with high precision, also by persons with reduced mobility. Thanks to VR goggles it is possible to transfer a patient, who often moves in a wheelchair, in the course of rehabilitation into practically any environment and conduct therapy without any barriers or risk factors which at this stage of rehabilitation would be impossible to overcome in the patient’s real world. However, currently we need a lot of research concerning efficacy, safety and satisfaction of the patients using this type of therapy. It is necessary in order to design detailed indications and contraindications, which will enable to precisely describe the best moment of introduction VR goggles therapy in neurological rehabilitation, the dose and the necessary safety precautions.

8. Conclusions 1. Analysis of the literature confirms the efficacy of virtual reality in functional recovery of patients after a stroke. It also confirms that depression frequently affects stroke survivors and significantly impedes the rehabilitation process. At the same time there is a lot of scientific evidence of high effectiveness of virtual therapy in treating psychological problems and mental disorders. 2. There is a shortage of VR-based technological solutions that would, beside physical rehabilitation, offer stroke patients therapeutic tools to alleviate psychological disturbance and improve the patient’s mood and motivation. A properly designed technology using VR goggles may activate the patient’s physical recovery, as well as offer psychological support. 3. Therapies using VR goggles will most likely become a field of intensive research involving specialists in many fields of technological, medical and psychological sciences. The research will have to take into account aspects like efficacy,safetyandsatisfactionofthepatientsusing this type of therapy.
